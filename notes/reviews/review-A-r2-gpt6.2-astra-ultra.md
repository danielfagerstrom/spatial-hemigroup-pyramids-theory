# Referee report

Spatial scale space from hemigroup axioms: the theory of image pyramids

Review of the supplied manuscript and supplement · 23 November 2026

## 1. Overall assessment and recommendation

Major revision. I would invite a revision centered on the finite-stage characterization and the obstruction to isotropy at every positive scale. The former distinguishes what an observer can infer from a stored ladder from what a principle imposed at every starting scale determines. The latter survives a genuinely weaker definition of a carrying family than the one used for the classification. The printed proofs support both results. The paper nevertheless misidentifies established probability results as part of its contribution, lists questions as open that its own arguments or short counterexamples settle, and makes incompatible statements about the class of families under review. The numerical magnitudes I could check are sound. The formal-verification claim is unusually explicit in the body, but the artifact identified as public was unavailable, and several errors concern exactly the prose outside its stated scope. These are material changes to the case for publication, even though the principal theorems can stand.

Page references are to the supplied PDFs, with S denoting a supplement page. I checked the printed arguments, compared the released earlier papers where identified below, and independently evaluated the numerical examples. I did not run the authors’ code or certify a Lean build. I distinguish errors requiring correction from possible improvements in §8.

## 2. Headline claims against the results

Bounded support (pp. 1, 3, 39–40, 107–108). Proposition 3.22 is correct and covers every increment, including a non-octave increment, of a jointly continuous convolution hemigroup of probability laws on Euclidean space. Symmetry, nondegeneracy, isotropy and dilation covariance play no role. “Any family continuous in scale” drops the indispensable cascade. For instance, the uniform laws on [−(t−s),t−s], with the point mass on the diagonal, are jointly weakly continuous. The abstract opens in a hemigroup setting, but the unqualified sentence and the repeated explanation on p. 108 invite a false reading. Negative filter weights are excluded by positivity before this argument begins. The point-mass exception must also survive “every finite mask” on p. 107.

Classification and ladders (pp. 1–3, 23, 27–38, 107). The three possible maximal covariance groups and the three nested classes for a specified q are different assertions. A family with maximal group r to the integer powers belongs to the middle class for q = rᵏ, k a positive integer. A fully covariant family belongs there for every q. “Every kernel is semi-selfdecomposable” means every origin kernel μₜ = μ0,t, with an infinitely divisible residual at that span. It does not apply to every stage μs,t. The ladder fixes the origin kernels at the knots, given the continuity at zero. It generally admits multiple interpolations, and inequivalence even after a change of clock needs the additional splitting argument. The claim on p. 3 that these families cannot be read off from the line is misleading. Every isotropic family is determined by one coordinate marginal at all scales (Proposition 3.16). Deciding which line families extend to a given dimension is the issue. Dimension dependence is present for the fully covariant exponents too.

The line and its grids (pp. 2–3, 34, 45–55, 100–101, 108). Propositions 4.5 and 4.7 justify the criterion and the sampled-Laplace example, and the disagreement with normalized samples of the finite continuum step at every level n ≥ 2. At n = 1 there is equality. The extension criterion makes

sense for every real q > 1. Actual scalar decimation on nested full-rank grids requires qΓ ⊂ Γ, hence integer q in a lattice basis. One can draw or dilate the mask at any real q without obtaining that sampling algorithm. The equivalent weights Wₙ describe the step from 1 to qⁿ, whereas μ0,qn includes the smoothing below scale 1. Figure 1 says this well. The introduction and discussion should retain the distinction whenever interpreting the stored levels.

Selection (pp. 1, 3–5, 78–83, 107–108). Theorem 5.19 proves uniqueness among isotropic q-covariant nondegenerate families, modulo increasing changes of clock χ with χ(qt) = qχ(t). In the similarity clock the heat-step parameter is c²t². In an arbitrary permitted clock it is χ(t)² after absorbing the choice of units. The test concerns every t > 0 and every real Schwartz input to that stage, at a weak spatial extremum of the output, comparing with the input at the same point. Tests on one image, on the integer-indexed stored rungs only, or only on inputs attained from a particular finer level are weaker. The abstract rightly says “from every scale.” It should also say which relabellings preserve the q clock. The radial splitting statement added to Proposition 3.20(2) supplies exactly the isotropic counterexamples needed for the one-ladder assertion.

Level differences and extrema (pp. 2–5, 71–78, 98–100, 107). The identity uₜ − uqt = −c²t² Δuqt is an exact identity between two continuum records before sampling. Burt and Adelson subtract an expanded coarse record after reduction. An identity for the former does not identify the latter algorithm’s residual as this Laplacian. The counts in Proposition 5.15 are existential in the input, with at least two later local maxima. One may choose an input for each prescribed Matérn or frame stage. The three grid examples concern the stated finite symmetric neighbourhoods containing the axial neighbours, integer q ≥ 2, and a possibly different input at each chosen level. Proposition 6.12 uses the tensor power of the specified five-tap binomial. Figure 4 illustrates a Bessel stage. The nearby Gaussian reference on p. 2 points to the wrong illustration.

Exactness and isotropy (pp. 1, 4, 84–95, 100–101, 107–108). Proposition 6.7 gives the stated equivalence for a symmetric, nontrivial probability mask on an infinite lattice, with infinite divisibility as a law on Euclidean space. A finite crop or a finite periodic group is a different mathematical object. Proposition 6.5 indeed excludes full rotation invariance of even one μ0,t, t > 0, in d ≥ 2, for every carrier in Definition 6.3. It does not say that the kernel law itself is countably carried, that all lattice locations have unequal weights at the same radius, or that directional error has a positive lower bound. “Exact or isotropic” suggests two independent options although the definition of isotropy here uses the carrying family, as p. 85 acknowledges. The symmetric carrier supplied by the existence proof does not confer symmetry on every carrier.

Verification (pp. 1, 5–7, 109–113). The abstract carefully says “formal counterparts.” Ten counts grouped cited results, fifteen counts domain-specific axiom declarations, and one counts a particular missing instantiation of mask membership. Neither the comparison of formal to printed statements nor the numerical computations and connecting arguments are covered by that single exception. The abstract should give a reader this boundary before relying on the count. Details are in §5 of this report.

## 3. Mathematical correctness

### Increments, strata and the ladder

Lemmas 3.3–3.5 (pp. 20–23). On a compact time interval joint continuity on the diagonal makes the row of increments of an increasingly fine partition uniformly infinitesimal. The cascade fixes its row-sum law. The null-array theorem therefore applies to an arbitrary interval. The new symmetric representation is justified by reflecting the triplet in the symmetric truncation convention, invoking uniqueness, and integrating the combined sine-minus-compensator term, which is integrable and odd. Its removal leaves the cosine exponent with Gaussian matrix A equal to half Sato’s. Conversely those data define the required law. Adding exponents gives additivity of Gaussian forms and of Lévy measures. Cancellation is legitimate on annuli of finite mass followed by exhaustion. This proves the order and uniqueness used in 3.5, also when there are infinitely many small jumps. The parenthetical justification on p. 46 that the compensator integrates an odd function is insufficient on its own, since that term alone need not be integrable before lattice finiteness has been proved. Use Lemma 3.4 directly there.

Theorems 3.12 and 3.15 (pp. 23–30). The first class has increasing rotation-invariant data, nonzero increments on every strict interval and continuity of each frequency exponent. The q-class adds a(qt) = q²a(t) and νqt = Dqνₜ in a discrete covariance clock. The full class is the self-decomposable radial exponent evaluated in a similarity clock, with the decreasing radial profile of Theorem 2.11 and an unrestricted scalar Gaussian part. The converse in 3.12(1) constructs valid increments. Specify zero initial data if it is also supposed to recover the given origin data. A fixed-radius compound Poisson Lévy measure fails the dilation domination at that sphere for every q > 1. The geometric train of uniform shell measures has the required integrability and positive differences, yet charges a sphere, impossible for a self-decomposable polar measure with radial factor k(r)dr/r. These witnesses separate the classes for every d, including d = 1. A local error in Proposition 3.14 is the claim that the weight of each fixed sphere hqⁱ rises from wᵢ to wᵢ₋₁ “over each octave.” On [qⁿ,qⁿ⁺¹] the indices for that sphere are i−n and i−n−1.

Lifting and dimension (pp. 30–33). In Proposition 3.17, projection of a uniform sphere onto a coordinate has an absolutely continuous interior density proportional, in unit-radius coordinates, to (1−u²) raised to (d−3)/2. The endpoints have zero mass even for d = 2. Integrate over radii and restrict the image Lévy measure off zero. A nonzero atom of the line Lévy measure is thus impossible, irrespective of atoms of the probability law itself. In 3.18 projection preserves the convolution factorization and infinite divisibility of the residual. The witness ∑i≤1 σqi gains precisely the shell σq on dilation of its predecessor, and charges the sphere of radius q. A projected uniform sphere in d+1 assigns that sphere zero mass because fixing the projected radius fixes at most two values of the missing coordinate. The strict inclusions are consequently proved for the classes of radial exponents in the statement. On p. 32 distinguish the jump-at-a-knot examples from continuous nondegenerate families, as on pp. S15–S17.

Proposition 3.20 (pp. 34–39). Iterating the refinement equation gives a product of the factors ŵ(q⁻ᵐξ), m ≥ 1, with a remainder evaluated at q⁻ᴹξ tending to 1. The knot has Gaussian part Aw/(q²−1) and jump measure ∑ Dq−mνw. The remainder measure tends to zero on sets bounded away from zero. The integrability test for the sum is ∫ ∑ₘ min(1,q⁻²ᵐ|v|²) νw(dv). The weight is comparable to |v|² near zero and to 1 + log |v| at large radius. This proves the exact logarithmic-moment threshold, including the pure Gaussian case. The g-moment theorem transfers the tail condition to the law. In the divergent case a bounded jump sum near frequency zero would force the contracted jump measure to satisfy Lévy integrability by averaging over a ball. Conversely boundedness on a set of positive measure yields such a neighbourhood by the cosine difference estimate and Steinhaus’s

theorem. This proves decay almost everywhere, even with a nonzero Gaussian part. Proposition 3.21 gives a reason to preserve this qualification at nonzero resonant frequencies. In (2), changing one common interpolation weight shows nonuniqueness with a fixed clock. On disjoint nonzero jump components two different time profiles can force contradictory values for a prospective common relabelling. If the components and the step are rotation invariant, so are the data at each phase and all resulting increments. This suffices for both revised downstream uses.

A missing hypothesis (pp. 35, 37). Proposition 3.20(4)(b) describes a radial Gaussian part and radial Lévy measure “so that” the law is rotation invariant. Those two data do not determine the drift. A translated centered Gaussian has exactly those data and generally a complex, nonradial transform. Explicitly assume a symmetric infinitely divisible law or prescribe zero drift in the stated truncation. If the phrase was intended as a separate assumption, write it as one. Carry |e| = 1 through the last ladder assertion as well, since evaluating the radial exponent at r e gives the spatially rescaled ladder for general |e|. These are repairs to the statement, independent of the sound product criterion.

Propositions 3.22–3.23 and 4.5 (pp. 39–42, 45–48). The support obstruction follows immediately once infinite divisibility of each increment is obtained. A refinable bounded law can still solve the knot equation. Calling the cubic spline a “knot kernel” without this qualification on p. 40 contradicts the subsequent exclusion. The line criterion’s converse now correctly uses Proposition 3.20(2) to produce the carrier and the line lemmas to read its folded data. For the one unformalized instance, the coefficients 4⁻ᵏ times the binomial coefficients of order 2k are symmetric, sum to 1 and are positive at more than one site for k ≥ 1. A centered cardinal B-spline of degree r ≥ 2 is even, supported on [−(r+1)/2,(r+1)/2], and positive on its interior. Its samples at 0 and ±1 survive normalization and only finitely many do. This verifies the printed step. Preserve the degree restriction and distinguish samples, the refinement coefficients, and the continuous spline density. The particular five-tap instance and the general support obstruction have different formal status.

### The selection and creation of extrema

Propositions 5.18–5.20 (pp. 78–84). I find the stage lemma convincing, including its necessity direction. Damped finite cosine sums give Schwartz tests. A definite quadratic form of their coefficients produces a strict maximum for the limiting test and nearby interior maxima for its approximants. An invisible frequency could be added without changing the limiting output and would violate the inequality at the input, excluding a zero of the multiplier. Testing relations between the rank-one forms ξξᵀ, with a small definite perturbation and both signs, makes 1/μ(̂ ξ)−1 a linear functional on their span, hence ξᵀCξ. The form is positive semidefinite. Conversely the Hessian test on u−tr(C D²u) = f gives the two required inequalities, including for singular C. Isotropy forces C = τI. The identity is the τ = 0 case. In d = 1 retain the symmetry hypothesis of Definition 5.17, since SO(1) alone imposes none.

For Theorem 5.19, one selected rung determines the whole stored ladder and its product at the knots. The Bessel stage has a nonzero radial jump measure that can be split into two nonzero radial restrictions, so 3.20(2) genuinely supplies inequivalent isotropic interpolations. Requiring the test at every t now yields the Bessel multiplier for each starting phase. Telescoping down to zero identifies μₜ with the same product at argument √τ(t)|ξ|. Continuity, the cascade bound on characteristic functions, and nondegeneracy turn √τ(t) into an increasing homeomorphism with the required q scaling. No differentiability of the clock is needed. Two octaves composed need not have the one-step property. The continuum generator and projection results on pp. 58–72 require care with their domains. An origin kernel may be smooth although one Bessel stage is singular at the spatial origin, and a bound on extrema of one-dimensional projections does not bound planar maxima.

Propositions 5.15–5.16 and 6.11–6.12 (pp. 73–78, 98–100). The narrow positive ridge gives a one-maximum input. The smoothed Gaussian bump has a strict central maximum by the nonnegative transform, positive near frequency zero. Absolute continuity makes the convolved ridge uniformly small by splitting off a bounded-density part and controlling the rest by its mass. Two separated balls then retain interior output maxima. This proves existence of at least two weak local maxima of a single output and the asserted separation of a superlevel set. The Matérn/frame reachability argument may use signed Schwartz initial inputs. The Gaussian mixture in clause (4) is positive. On p. 76 the reciprocal Gaussian transfer exp(a|ξ|²) outgrows exp(ε|ξ|²) only for ε < a, which makes “every” wrong.

The lattice proof places a positive ridge and two separated peaks relative to a line that carries less than all mask mass. Choosing the separation on the retained grid preserves two coarse maxima. For an infinitely divisible symmetric lattice law, finiteness of the jump measure yields a signed summable convolution inverse by the exponential series for −ν. Zero extension from retained sites and that inverse give surjectivity on bounded records at each preceding level. The log moment is unnecessary for this inverse. The five-tap binomial has zeros in its full transfer, so this argument cannot be reused verbatim. Its subsampled mask at q = 2 has the invertible three coefficients (1,6,1)/16, and at integer q ≥ 3 only the center survives. This supplies the separate reachability proof. The neighbourhood assumptions, the distinction between local counts and extremal values, and the initial-input quantifiers matter.

Two small qualifications on pp. 74 and 81 are worth making explicit. In fact the one-step construction in 5.15(1) appears to need only absolute continuity. Indeed W = μ ∗ B is positive at zero and tends uniformly to zero at infinity, so one can choose a large sphere on which W is below W(0)/4, separate the translated spheres sufficiently, and then suppress the ridge by precisely the printed bound. This gives an interior maximum in each ball without the Fourier hypothesis or a claim that W peaks at zero. Also, in 5.19(3) give the separate assertion about μ0,t the explicit domain t > 0, since μ₀,₀ is the identity and has the principle.

### Lattice support and the revised grid proofs

Lemma 3.8 (p. 24). Apply the one-dimensional lattice result to every coordinate in a dual basis for Γ. The pushforward is restricted off zero, so an individual projection cannot see jumps in its kernel. Intersecting all the integer-coordinate conditions puts the original jumps on Γ, while the dual directions and positive semidefiniteness force the whole Gaussian matrix to vanish. A lattice has a gap around zero, so its Lévy measure is finite. A drift remains possible in the general lattice lemma. Symmetry of the mask removes it where needed.

Lemma 6.4 and Propositions 6.5, 6.7 (pp. 87–95). Weak continuity and the cascade imply infinite divisibility for all increments even under the weaker grid definition. The knot equation iterates, its remainder tends to 1, and the symmetric mask factors force a symmetric knot law. It is now legitimate to identify its unique symmetric data, including the infinite sum of contracted jump measures. Consecutive knot laws have zero Gaussian part. Their Lévy measures bound the measure at each intervening time from below and above, so it is nonzero and carried by the countable union of dilated lattices. Such a measure has an atom off zero. Rotation invariance would give equally massive atoms on an entire sphere, impossible for a Lévy measure finite away from zero. If the mask spans, the images of independent atoms lie in finite sets of atoms of sufficient mass on their spheres, which gives the finite orthogonal group. On p. 90 “R fixes only 0” is false, even for the identity. Invertibility gives the needed statement that only 0 maps to 0.

For the frame example, expanding the logarithms of the two geometric factors on p. 47 gives folded weights 2aⁿ/n, n > 0. Unfolding puts a to the power |n| divided by |n| at each signed site, and the knot

product contracts and sums these atoms along the coordinate axes, giving (6.2) and precisely the signed permutations at the knots. For 6.7, Lemma 6.4 gives the necessary log moment for any carrier, and 3.20(2) gives a symmetric carrier for the converse. Proposition 6.14 on pp. 101–102 now legitimately uses 3.23(2) to identify the limit. The product of the dyadically contracted five-tap transfers tends to sinc⁴(hξ/2), the transform of the sum of four centered uniforms. Tensorization preserves the refinement identity and the nontrivial bounded support that forbids embedding. The binomial example in S.38 also distinguishes the limiting standardized fourth cumulant, −3/10, from the Gaussian value and proves variation diminution for the discrete differences (pp. S40–S42).

False scope statement (pp. 84, 87–89, 100–101, 108). Definition 6.3 imposes neither intermediate symmetry nor nondegeneracy on every interval. For an explicit carrier start with the symmetric one, fix v ≠ 0 and put b(t) = t sin(2π logq t)v for t > 0 and b(0) = 0. Translate every increment by b(t)−b(s). The translations telescope, vary continuously, satisfy b(qt) = qb(t), and vanish at every knot. Thus (a)–(d) and every prescribed grid step survive, but generic intermediate increments are asymmetric. The equivalence with the stronger notion on the line concerns the masks that admit a carrier. Statements applying the symmetric classification to every such carrier or saying all families studied are symmetric are wrong.

### Four assertions of openness

Gaussian maximum creation (pp. 76, 108). Proposition 5.15(4) already answers §7.2(1) affirmatively as worded. In its finite chain let j be the first record with at least two local maxima. Every preceding record is a positive Gaussian mixture tending to zero at infinity and therefore has at least one global maximum. Record j−1 has exactly one local maximum. Set τ equal to its variance parameter. The same positive input then has the requested counts at τ and q²τ. Spatial dilation puts the pair at any prescribed initial variance if desired. Locating j for the specific plotted or constructed input would be a different question.

A principle after decimation (pp. 84, 92–93, 108–109). For the natural pointwise version of Definition 5.17 the answer is negative, even for a nearest-neighbour Helmholtz resolvent. Prescribe a fine output u with u(0) = 1, u(±heⱼ) = 2 for each axis, and zero elsewhere. Define f = (I−τΔₕ)u, τ > 0. The resolvent applied to f returns u. For integer q ≥ 2 the restriction of u has a strict maximum at 0, yet f(0) = 1−2dτ/h² < u(0). Adding a constant makes the input positive if required. For the frame mask, the global output maximum used in Proposition 6.6 already survives restriction. If the author intends comparison with a different coarse representation of the input, that operator and the corresponding inequality need a definition before an open problem can be stated.

Similarities (pp. 4, 88–90, 108–109). The restriction to scalar q is accurate for the printed theorem. The mathematical question in §7.2(3), under the indicated analogue of (a)–(d), has the same negative answer for M = qR. At the knot, μ₁ is the inverse-M image of μ₁ ∗ w. Iteration leaves a remainder tending to the identity law, since M contracts by q⁻¹ in reverse. Its jump measure is the sum of the pushforwards of νw by the maps M⁻ᵐ, m ≥ 1. On sets outside a ball the remainder is bounded by the tail of ν₁ outside a ball expanding by qᵏ after k iterations. The knot Gaussian matrix satisfies MAMᵀ = A since the lattice mask has none, hence q² tr(A) = tr(A) and A = 0. Sandwiching between consecutive knots again gives a nonzero countably carried jump measure at every positive scale. A rotation of the lattice at each level does not change the countability argument. This establishes the isotropy conclusion, without claiming a Lean counterpart or a general result for arbitrary expansive matrices.

Isotropic refinable limits of finite masks (pp. 85, 101–102). Even without infinite divisibility there is a short obstruction in d ≥ 2 for the stated scalar refinement equation. Suppose μ is a rotation-invariant refinable probability law. Its transform is radial and nonzero on a ball about zero, so ŵ(ξ) = μ(̂ qξ)/μ̂(ξ) is radial there. For a finitely supported mask ŵ is real analytic on the entire

frequency space. For each rotation R, analytic uniqueness extends ŵ(Rξ) = ŵ(ξ) from that ball to all ξ. Hence w is rotation invariant. A finite support containing a nonzero point cannot contain its spherical orbit. The sole rotation-invariant finite probability mask is δ₀. This question has a definite answer quite apart from exact pyramids.

## 4. Numerical examples and reproducibility

Table 1 and Figure 3 (pp. 48–55). The comparison attributed to grid_pyramid_exactness.py measures the error in the implemented cascade directly. One comparator performs the discrete recurrence and decimates after every stage. The other evaluates the finite continuum convolution at the final lattice sites and normalizes there. Half the ℓ¹ distance compares probability arrays and is sensitive to the transfer of mass into the central cell from nearby cells. I independently reproduced all twelve printed entries at their stated precision. At h/(ct) = 1 the distances for levels 2, 3 and 4 were 0.06252, 0.06725 and 0.06597. At 0.1 they were 0.0005668, 0.0003713 and 0.0003304. The reported factors of about 110 and 200 follow. The coefficient-ratio proof on pp. 53–54 establishes inequality for every spacing and every n ≥ 2. Twelve numbers establish neither a convergence rate nor a uniform bound in level, and the text appropriately disclaims such a bound. Single-kernel aliased mass in 4.7(3) measures a different error.

To make the last digits reproducible, specify whether the initial 2¹⁶+1-site window retains its physical extent or its site count after each decimation, how the recursive passes are initialized at the two ends, and precisely when each array is normalized. Give a window-doubling check or a bound for the omitted tails, separately from roundoff in the recurrence and partial fractions. The order-of-magnitude cutoff in the caption is not a certificate of machine precision for the whole calculation.

Sampled Gaussians (p. 55). The 4096-site periodic test attributed to sampled_gaussian_increments.py is specified well enough to reproduce the substantial negative coefficients, approximately −0.01093 and −0.004601 at ±2 for the first two pairs, and approximately −2.82 × 10⁻⁷ at ±10 for (2,2.5). For (0.7,3.0) the printed minimum is at the double-precision noise floor. Higher-precision evaluation on the same grid gives a coefficient about −2.82 × 10⁻³³ at ±36. The printed calculation cannot certify a sign for this pair, and four pairs with differing relative widths cannot establish a trend over all scales. A finite periodic inverse transform is also a different test from an infinite-lattice positivity assertion.

There is a short rigorous repair for the important infinite-lattice conclusion. Let X and Z have normalized integer Gaussian samples at widths 0.5 and 0.6. A positive convolution increment would give Z in law as X+Y with X,Y independent, E Y = 0 and Y integer valued. Then E Y² = E Z² − E X² is between 0.136 and 0.137, while E Y⁴ = E Z⁴ − E X⁴ − 6 E X² E Y² is between 0.015 and 0.017. These intervals follow by summing |k| ≤ 5 with weights exp(−k²/(2σ²)). At σ ≤ 0.6 the omitted unnormalized bilateral fourth-moment tail is below 10⁻¹⁸ by a geometric bound from k = 6. Finite fourth moments of X and Z imply that of Y. But Y⁴ ≥ Y² on the integers, a contradiction. One such pair proves the stated failure, without interpreting small negative Fourier coefficients as certified signs.

should retain that quantifier where the text leaves the example. For Figure 5’s middle panel I get masses at (5,0) and (3,4), in lattice units, of 0.00035882 and 0.00025445, ratio 1.410. ## 5. Trust base and the six unproved background statements

What the inventory says (pp. 5–7, 109–113). The arithmetic is internally consistent. Of 27 numbered source interfaces, ten are admitted as fifteen domain axiom declarations, six inherited from AffineHemigroup and nine declared locally. ## 6. Novelty and relation to prior work

The closest omitted theorem (pp. 1–4, 25–26, 34–38, 93–95, 107). Maejima and Ueda, Proposition 3.2, Theorem 3.3 and equation (3.4), pp. 5–6 of the preprint, already give the log-moment domain, the infinite product and the unique semi-selfdecomposable law associated with an infinitely divisible innovation. In their notation set b = q and take their residual to be the image of the present mask under x ↦ x/q. Their product starts at power zero and the manuscript’s at power minus one, so the two products then coincide factor for factor. Their result already holds in several dimensions without symmetry. The earlier Maejima–Naito paper and Sato §15 supply the class and the factor condition. Maejima–Sato’s semi-selfsimilar additive processes, especially the one-period construction in Theorem 7, p. 361, are close precedents for extending the data on one octave, as p. 26 acknowledges. The probability content of the knot criterion should be attributed explicitly. The imaging interpretation, data between the knots, inequivalence under relabelling, and use of that freedom in the selection theorem are meaningful further contributions. Maejima and Ueda · Maejima and Naito · Maejima and Sato

Classical pyramids (pp. 1–4, 34, 39–42, 72, 107, S35). Burt (1981), especially Observation 1, p. 24 and (5), p. 25, explicitly studies the repeated equivalent kernels and their rescaled characteristic shape. Burt and Adelson (1983), pp. 533–535, use the same generating weights across levels, discuss equivalent weighting functions and define both reduction and expansion. Consequently the general statement on p. 1 that the design literature leaves the whole family open is historically misleading. The additional requirement here is a jointly continuous two-time positive extension through all the scaled masks. Meer, Baugher and Rosenfeld optimize generating kernels for decimation and reconstruction losses, which supports the narrower contrast. Discrete scale space and causal kernels (pp. 4–5, 29, 55–56, 65, 107, S5–S6, S38–S39). That says nothing adverse about its scale-space property on its own grid or a comparison in which the grid spacing is also changed. A geometric cascade of first-order factors convolved with its reflection has transfer equal to the squared modulus, precisely the product of reciprocal quadratic factors after matching units. Regularization and diffusion (pp. 4, 56–58, 71–72, 107, 115). Hanke and Groetsch already have products of residual factors α/(α+λ) with geometric regularization parameters.
