Manuscript: *Spatial scale space from hemigroup axioms: the theory of image pyramids* (119 pp.) and its supplement (46 pp.). Page numbers are pages of the PDF; supplement pages are written S-n.

## 1. Overall assessment and recommendation (for the block)

**Recommendation: major revision.**

The mathematics of the block stands. I found no false numbered statement and no gap in a printed proof in Sections 2 and 3: Theorems 2.2, 2.3, 2.9, 2.16, 2.22, 2.27, Lemmas 3.2 to 3.6, 3.8 to 3.10, 3.16, Theorems 3.12 and 3.15, and Propositions 3.13, 3.14, 3.17 to 3.23 are correct as printed, and the statements outside the block that the abstract leans on (the stage lemma, the selection theorem, the lattice criterion, the two obstructions to isotropy) are correct too. The revision is needed elsewhere. (i) The abstract and Section 1 are in two places out of step with the body: they never say that the selected family is covariant under every dilation, which the body proves and which changes how the selection reads, and the contributions list still says that a dilation which turns the lattice is "outside the statement" although Proposition 6.6 and Section 7.2 now answer it. (ii) Most of the structure theory of Section 3 is the classical theory of additive processes and of semi-selfdecomposable laws, read for scale spaces. The text says so in part, but several statements, and the abstract's "classification" and lattice criterion, are presented without the attribution a reader of the probability literature would expect, and the nearest precedent in the imaging literature (Lindeberg's time-causal limit kernel, which the paper itself identifies on p. 66 as the mixing law of its Matérn pyramid) is absent from Section 1. (iii) The account of the trust base is honest in Section 1.1 but is contradicted by the sentence that closes most proofs, and it cannot be audited: neither the formal statements nor the fifteen axioms are printed, and by p. 117 the repository does not exist yet. (iv) The isotropy statements are correct but their scope is narrower than the framing suggests; I give a lattice mask with a rotation-invariant refinable law in 2.7 below. (v) The block is far too long for a journal: it has 41 pages, 13 of them restate cited material, several more repeat the same summary, and the first statement that I take to be new mathematics and not a reading of known theory (Proposition 3.17) comes on p. 32.

## 2. The headline claims against what is proved

For each claim: the statement that carries it, whether it carries it, and what the headline omits.

**2.1 "The isotropic families form three nested strata" (abstract; contribution 1, p. 2).** Carried by Lemma 3.6(2), Theorem 3.12 and Theorem 3.15, all correct. Two remarks.

- The abstract says the paper "gives a classification". For stratum 2 what is proved is a rewriting of the definition in Lévy coordinates: Theorem 3.12(2) is Lemma 3.9, an equivalence that follows at once from the uniqueness of the Lévy–Khintchine pair, and the content is the synthesis Lemma 3.10 (any data on one octave that grow, are continuous and glue extend to a family), which p. 26 itself attributes, for processes, to Maejima and Sato. A parametrization by paths over one octave is not a classification in the sense of the earlier papers, where the families are listed by one exponent and a gauge. "Description" is the accurate word; Section 3.2 uses it, the abstract does not.
- The strata are relative to a fixed q (Definition 3.11), while the trichotomy of Lemma 3.6(2) is intrinsic to the family. A family whose group is generated by the square root of q is strictly in stratum 2 for q, and one whose group is q^Z is strictly in stratum 1 for the square root of q. Page 28 handles this correctly; the sentence of p. 2 ("so there are three cases ... The families therefore form three nested strata") runs the two together.

**2.2 "In the middle stratum the kernel from the scale 0 to each scale is semi-selfdecomposable" (abstract).** Lemma 3.9, correct.

**2.3 "The ladder of octave steps ... determines the kernels at the knots" and the criterion "infinitely divisible with a logarithmic moment" (abstract; p. 3).** Proposition 3.20(1) to (3), correct; I re-derived the product formula, the data A_1 = A_ϱ/(q² − 1) and ν_1 = Σ D_{q^-m} ν_ϱ, the bound (3.2), the uniform and split couplings, and the three steps of clause (3). The qualification that the headline omits is one of attribution, not of mathematics: see 6.1. For laws, clauses (1) and (2) are the Maejima–Ueda characterization, as p. 35 says; the divergence without the moment, clause (3), is older and more general than stated (6.1); and the family between the knots is Lemma 3.10, known for processes. What is new in the proposition is the non-uniqueness beyond a relabelling and clause (4).

**2.4 "No law of bounded support other than a point mass is a step of any family that obeys the cascade and is continuous in scale" (abstract; Proposition 3.22, p. 40).** The proposition proves exactly this. The qualifications are in the body and are fair: the steps are probability laws, so a mask with a negative weight is outside from the start (p. 3 says so), and "continuous in scale" is joint continuity of (s,t) ↦ transform on the closed set s ≤ t, with the diagonal μ_{t,t} = δ_0 included. What the headline does not convey is that the result is a two-line corollary of two textbook theorems: the laws of the increments of an additive process in law are infinitely divisible, and an infinitely divisible law other than a point mass has unbounded support (Sato, Theorem 9.1 and Corollary 24.4, from memory). Lemma 3.3 is the first of these with the standard proof (Khintchine's theorem on null arrays), and it is not attributed. The contribution is the reading for pyramids, and the abstract should not suggest more.

**2.5 "Up to a relabelling g of the scales with g(qt) = q g(t), it is the one isotropic family covariant under q whose octave steps from every scale obey the maximum principle of an implicit Euler step" (abstract; contribution 3, p. 4; Theorem 5.19(2) with Proposition 5.18).** I re-derived both. Step 1 of the converse of the stage lemma (pp. 81–82), the exclusion of zeros, and the polarization that gives 1/transform − 1 = ω·Cω are correct, and so is the recovery of the gauge χ in Theorem 5.19(2) (strict monotonicity from (ND), continuity from (I2), χ(q^n t) = q^n χ(t) from covariance). The claim is not stronger than the result. Two qualifications are missing from the headline.

- **The selected family is covariant under every dilation.** Theorem 5.4(1) states S_λ t = λt for every λ > 0, Theorem 5.19(2) ends "and it is fully covariant", p. 15 says "Both families are covariant under every dilation", and the table of p. 31 lists the Matérn pyramid as the example of stratum 3. Neither the abstract nor contribution 3 says so: both describe the result as a selection among "isotropic families covariant under q". A reader of the first five pages concludes that the paper has found a genuinely discrete-covariant family that the classical theory misses. What is proved is the opposite and more interesting: required of every octave step, the principle forces full covariance, and the selected family is a member of the class that the author's isotropic paper already classifies (F_q lies in A_d, Theorem 5.4(1)). This must be in the abstract.
- The principle is required at every weak local maximum and minimum of the output, for every real Schwartz input (Definition 5.17). It is the word "local" that does the work: at global extrema every resolvent of a symmetric Lévy semigroup has the property, and the converse of the stage lemma is in substance the statement that a translation-invariant operator with the positive maximum principle at local maxima is a second-order differential operator. The abstract's phrase "the maximum principle of an implicit Euler step" hides this; one clause would fix it (see also 6.4).

**2.6 "On a lattice, a symmetric probability mask other than a point mass has an exact grid pyramid if and only if it is infinitely divisible with a logarithmic moment" (abstract; Propositions 4.5(2) and 6.8).** Correct. It is Proposition 3.20 together with Lemma 6.4, which removes the symmetry of the carrying family; I checked Lemma 6.4 and the equivalence (a) to (c) of Proposition 6.8. The same attribution applies as in 2.3.

**2.7 "In d ≥ 2 no kernel at a positive scale of an exact grid pyramid whose mask is carried by a lattice is rotation invariant" (abstract; Propositions 6.5(2) and 6.6).** Both are correct. Lemma 3.8 is correct (I also checked that its cited input, Theorem 2.25, is true: the modulus of the transform of a law on Z is 2π-periodic, so the real part of the exponent is bounded, which gives A = 0 and a finite Lévy measure, and its vanishing at 2π puts the Lévy measure on Z). The proof of Proposition 6.6, the one proposition proved in print only, is complete: A_1 = M^-n A_1 (M^-n)^T tends to 0, the remainder M_*^-N ν_1 vanishes off every ball because |M^N x| ≥ |x| / ‖M^-N‖, the squeeze M_*^n ν_1 ≤ ν_t ≤ M_*^{n+1} ν_1 puts every ν_t on a countable set, and a rotation-invariant Lévy measure with an atom off 0 charges an uncountable sphere. Nothing is assumed of M beyond ‖M^-n‖ → 0, and neither symmetry of the family nor (ND) is used.

The scope, however, leaves a wrong impression in several places (p. 2 "It is called isotropic when some kernel of its family ... is rotation invariant"; p. 4 "a grid pyramid without one is not isotropic in this sense either"; pp. 86–87 and the title of Section 6, "Exact or isotropic on a lattice, not both"; p. 106 "The obstruction belongs to the lattice").

- Isotropy is defined through a carrying family, so for every pyramid that is not exact the statement "not isotropic" is true by definition and says nothing. The objects whose rotation invariance is denied are the kernels μ_t from scale zero, which, as the caption of Figure 1 says, no level of the algorithm applies. The paper is aware of this (p. 86, "The definition is deliberately weak"), but the contributions list is not written that way.
- For pyramids that are not exact the paper offers Proposition 6.16: a finite mask has no rotation-invariant refinable law. That is correct, and its proof uses only that the transform of the mask is real-analytic, so it holds verbatim for every mask with an exponential moment, the sampled Laplace and resolvent masks included. **Without a decay condition it fails.** Let Γ = hZ^d, q > 1, and let H be a continuous, radial, positive-definite function with H(0) = 1 and support in the ball of radius r_0 < 2π/(h(q+1)), for instance the normalized autocorrelation of the indicator of a ball. Let p ≥ 0 be the density with transform H and put w(n) := h^d p(hn). By Poisson summation the transform of w is the periodization Σ_k H(ω + 2πk/h), whose terms have disjoint supports, so w is a symmetric probability law on hZ^d, not δ_0, with a logarithmic moment (p decays like |x|^-(d+1); a smooth H gives a mask with all moments). Put μ^(ω) := Π_{m≥1} H(q^-m ω), the law of Σ q^-m Y_m with Y_m independent of density p. It is rotation invariant and not δ_0, its transform vanishes for |ω| ≥ q r_0, and for |ω| < q r_0 only the term k = 0 of the periodization is nonzero, so μ^(qω) = H(ω) μ^(ω) = w^(ω) μ^(ω) for every ω: D_q μ = μ ∗ w. So a nonnegative mask on the square lattice, of infinite support, has a rotation-invariant refinable law in every dimension, and its rescaled equivalent weights converge to it. The mask is not infinitely divisible (its transform has zeros), so nothing in the paper is contradicted. But the reader of Sections 1, 6 and 7.2 is left believing that a lattice excludes a rotation-invariant limit, and that is false. The answer to question 4 of Section 7.2 should state this boundary.

**2.8 The four answers of Section 7.2 (pp. 112–113).**

1. Chosen rung of the Gaussian ladder: matches Proposition 5.15(5); the reduction (4) to (5) by dilation of the input is correct.
2. Decimation: matches Proposition 5.21, which is correct but thin. After decimation the two arrays compared, u and f restricted to the coarse grid, are related by no operator, and the origin is a coarse-grid maximum only because the fine neighbours where u is larger have been discarded. "Read pointwise, no" is literally true; the answer should say that the principle is a statement about one smoothing step on one grid and has no content across a decimation.
3. Rotating dilation: matches Proposition 6.6. **But p. 4 still says "A pyramid whose dilation also turns the lattice is outside the statement (Section 7)."** That sentence was true of Proposition 6.5 and is now misleading: Section 7 answers the question. The abstract, for its part, mentions "the proposition excepted, on expanding dilation matrices" without ever saying what that proposition states.
4. Finite mask: matches Proposition 6.16; see 2.7 for what the answer omits.

**2.9 "machine-checked in all but one step and one proposition, from ten cited facts taken as axioms" (abstract) against Section 1.1.** The counts agree. Ten admitted facts (Theorems 2.15, 2.17, 2.19, 2.20, 2.21, 2.23, 2.24, 2.25, 2.26, 2.32) carry 3+1+1+3+1+1+1+1+2+1 = 15 axioms, as Table 2 lists; the 27 numbered theorems of Section 2 split as 10 admitted, 11 proved, 6 unread (2.7, 2.12, 2.13, 2.14, 2.30, 2.31); every numbered statement of Sections 3 to 6 and of the supplement has a row in Table 4 except Proposition 6.6. The abstract is consistent with Section 1.1. What it does not carry over: that two earlier formal developments of the author's are imported, six of the fifteen axioms living there; and that the comparison of formal with printed statements was made by AI agents only (p. 6, p. 116). The second is the weak link of the whole claim and belongs in the abstract's last sentence in so many words. See Section 5 of this report.

**F6 (pp. 35–37, Proposition 3.20(2)). The non-uniqueness is stated by a sufficient condition where an exact one is available.** A family through the ladder is a continuous increasing path of data from D* to D* + D_ϱ over the octave. If the data D_ϱ = (A_ϱ, ν_ϱ) span an extreme ray of the cone of admissible data, every such path is c(t) D_ϱ and the family is the uniform coupling relabelled by g(t) = q^{c(t)}; if not, D_ϱ = D_a + D_b with D_a, D_b not proportional and the split coupling of the proof is not a relabelling. So the families through a ladder are unique up to G_q exactly when the step is Gaussian of rank one with no jumps, or has no Gaussian part and Lévy measure c(δ_x + δ_-x); among isotropic families, exactly when the step is Gaussian or its Lévy measure is a multiple of one σ_r with no Gaussian part. The proposition's condition (ν_ϱ a sum of two parts on disjoint sets) misses the split between a Gaussian part and a jump part, and the reader cannot tell from it that the Gaussian ladder, alone among those that matter here, has a unique isotropic family up to relabelling. This is a strengthening to make, not an error.

## 4. The numerical examples (the part that lies in the block)

**Figure 2 (p. 39).** The design is sound and the caption gives the cut-offs (m ≤ 89; a trapezoid rule on 4 × 10⁶ steps). Two points.

- The one conclusion drawn from it, that the period-one oscillation of P = Ψ_1 − log_q(h|ω|) "does not die out", is "read from the plot" (text and caption). A computation cannot show a statement about a limit, and here it need not: the statement is provable. With x = h|ω| and u = log_q x, the limit is P_∞(x) = Σ_{m≥1} (1 − J_0(x q^-m)) − Σ_{n≥0} J_0(x q^n) − log_q x, a continuous function of period 1 in u, and its k-th Fourier coefficient is g*(2πik / log q) / log q, where g*(s) = −2^{s−1} Γ(s/2) / Γ(1 − s/2) is the Mellin transform of 1 − J_0 continued to the imaginary axis. Its modulus is 1/(2π|k|) for every k ≠ 0. So the limit is not constant, its mean-square oscillation is 1/12 for every q, and the plotted amplitude (about ±0.5 around −0.8) agrees with that. I derived this by hand and have not checked it by machine; the author should verify it and then replace "read from the plot" by the statement.
- The caption names no script, unlike Table 1 (p. 55), and p. 117 says only that the figures "are computed by scripts from the formulas they state". Name it.

## 5. The trust base (Section 1.1, Appendix A)

Section 1.1 is clear and, read on its own, honest. It says which facts are admitted and under how many axioms, that "machine-checked" is said of the formal counterpart and "does not certify each step of the printed argument", that the prose and the numbers are outside, and that the comparison of formal with printed statements was made by AI agents. The counts are consistent with Appendix A (2.9 above), and pinning the axiom list of each headline declaration in the build (p. 113) is good practice. The following is wrong or missing.

**T1. The sentence that closes the proofs contradicts Section 1.1.** "Every step is machine-checked" stands at the end of nearly every proof of Sections 2 to 6 and of the supplement. Section 1.1 (pp. 5–6) says that it "means this of the formal counterpart of the statement proved" and not of the printed steps. A reader who opens the paper at a proof, as most will, reads a claim that the paper withdraws forty pages earlier. And the formal proofs demonstrably take other routes than the printed ones: F1 above (Theorem 3.12 does not read Theorem 2.20, its printed proof does, through Lemma 3.5); Proposition 5.15 against Theorem 5.4(1); S-23 ("The formal proof takes the jointly measurable density from Mathlib's Radon–Nikodym derivative of kernels, the fact read here from Kallenberg"); S-32 ("The formal proof reads ... as the real part of the coefficient j = 1"). The sentence has to say what is true: that the formal counterpart of the statement is machine-checked from the facts named.

**T2. The claim cannot be audited.** No formal statement is printed, apart from one axiom report (p. 7). By p. 117 "the repository, the tag and the DOI do not exist yet". By p. 116 "no human has reviewed the paper, and the formal statements in Lean have been reviewed by AI agents only". The abstract concedes that the match of each formal statement with its printed one is not machine-checked. Put together: the one link on which the value of the verification depends has been examined by no person, and a referee is given no means to examine it. F1 shows that the question is not idle. Two things are needed before the claim can stand in a journal: access to the development for the referees, and, in the paper, the Lean text of the fifteen axioms and of the headline declarations of Table 3. The axioms matter most. They are the trust base; a single one stated too strongly (a converse without its integrability hypothesis, a triplet without ν({0}) = 0) would make every check that reads it worthless, and nothing in the paper argues that the fifteen are jointly true as formalized.

**T3. "Each admitted fact is stated as its source states it, or narrower" (p. 7)** is not accurate for Theorem 2.25 (a conclusion taken from a proof) nor for Theorem 2.15 (the author's formulation, not Karlin's); see 3.3.

**T4. Table 4 (p. 115).** Its lead-in says that it covers "every numbered statement of the paper and of the supplement" and names two kinds of exception, the step of Proposition 4.5(4) and the six unread facts. Proposition 6.6 has no row and is not named there. The inventory should agree with Section 1.1.

**T5. The axiom names** cited_A42, cited_A43_…, cited_A46, cited_A22_polya (Table 2, p. 113) follow a ledger numbering that the paper nowhere explains.

**T6. Imported developments.** Six axioms and three theorems (2.8, 2.9, 2.11) come from the formal development of the affine paper, and the line classification (Theorem 2.5) from ScaleSpaceCore; both belong to preprints of the last three months by the same author. Section 1.1 says this; the abstract's "from ten cited facts" does not. A reader should be told in one sentence that the verification is relative to those developments as well.

**Is anything claimed as verified that the text elsewhere qualifies?** Yes, in the sense of T1, and in one more: the abstract counts "the numbered statements", definitions included (Table 4 lists them "with the statements of its section"). A definition has nothing to check; its formal counterpart is exactly where a mismatch with the printed text would hide. Say that the definitions were compared, not verified.

## 6. Novelty and relation to prior work

### 6.1 Additive processes, semi-selfdecomposable laws, hemigroups

A "family" of this paper is a convolution hemigroup of probability laws, weakly continuous in both parameters: the laws of the increments of an additive process in law, up to a change of time. Read so, a good part of Section 3 is classical, and the paper should say it statement by statement. (Numbers in Sato's book are from memory.)

- **Lemma 3.3** (the steps are infinitely divisible, pp. 21–22) is Sato's Theorem 9.1, with the same proof. Not attributed.
- **Lemma 3.5 and Theorem 3.12(1)** (the data vanish at 0, grow, are continuous; conversely such data give a family, pp. 22–23, 28) are the description of an additive process in law by its system of generating triplets, Sato's Theorems 9.7 and 9.8, in the symmetric and the rotation-invariant case. Not attributed.
- **Lemma 3.6(2)** (the ratios of covariance form a closed group: 1, the powers of one ratio, or all). The same dichotomy for the epochs of a semi-selfsimilar process opens Maejima and Sato's paper [24] (from memory). The setting differs (here the time change is arbitrary and the group is of space ratios), so the lemma needs its proof; a "cf." is due.
- **Lemma 3.9, Theorem 3.12(2), Lemma 3.10**: the correspondence between semi-selfsimilar additive processes and semi-selfdecomposable laws, and the construction from one period, are [24]. The paper says so for Lemma 3.10 (p. 26, S-10). Fair.
- **Proposition 3.20(1), (2)**: attributed to Maejima and Ueda on p. 5 and p. 35. As far as I can judge from memory the attribution is right: they characterize the semi-selfdecomposable laws of span b as the laws of Σ_{n≥0} b^-n (X_{n+1} − X_n) for a Lévy process X with a logarithmic moment, and the remark that their product starts one factor earlier is correct, the residual being D_{q^-1} μ_{1,q}. But the abstract and the contributions list (pp. 1, 3, 4) state the criterion three times without it.
- **Proposition 3.20(3)** (no limit without the moment) is older and holds in greater generality: a series Σ q^-m Y_m of independent, identically distributed Y_m converges in law if and only if E log^+ |Y| is finite, with no symmetry and no infinite divisibility (Wolfe 1982; Vervaat 1979; from memory). The proof by Steinhaus's theorem is pleasant and self-contained, but the statement is not attributed, and its natural generality matters here: it says that a mask, exact or not, has a refinable law if and only if it has a logarithmic moment, which is the statement Section 3.5 wants beside Proposition 3.23.
- **Proposition 3.22** is Sato's Theorem 9.1 and Corollary 24.4 in two lines (2.4 above).
- **The hemigroup literature is not cited at all.** "Hemigroup" is the standard word for these two-parameter convolution families. If my memory is right, Becker-Kern (Stable and semistable hemigroups: domains of attraction and self-decomposability, J. Theoret. Probab., 2003) studies exactly hemigroups covariant under every dilation and under the powers of one, in operator generality, and ties them to self-decomposable and semi-selfdecomposable laws. That would be the nearest prior work to Section 3, by name and by content. I could not check it; the author must, and must position Theorems 3.12 and 3.15 against it.

What I take to be new in the block: Proposition 3.17 and Proposition 3.18 (what the line does not decide, and the strict shrinking with the dimension under one ratio); the non-uniqueness beyond a relabelling in Proposition 3.20(2); Proposition 3.20(4); Proposition 3.19, which is elementary; and the reading of all of it for pyramids, which is the paper's real contribution and is worth having.

### 6.2 The imaging literature

- **Burt (1981), Burt and Adelson (1983).** Used accurately as far as I can check from memory: the four constraints on the generating kernel (p. 1), the equivalent weighting function (p. 35), the convergence to a characteristic shape. The reading "run downward in scale" is the paper's and is a good one. Proposition 3.23(1), that no equivalent weight is the dilate of the one before, is a fair comment on Burt's observation.
- **Meer, Baugher and Rosenfeld (1987).** "require it to be good for one decimation" (p. 1) is too loose to check. Say which criterion.
- **Lindeberg (1990).** Placed in stratum 1 and in no stratum 2 (Proposition 3.13 at d = 1, the remark after it on hZ^d, Proposition S.5). Correct.
- **Missing from Section 1: Lindeberg's time-causal limit kernel.** It is the one construction in the scale-space literature that is covariant under the powers of one ratio in place of every dilation: an infinite convolution of first-order kernels with geometrically spaced time constants, covariant under temporal scalings by integer powers of the distribution parameter. The paper knows it. Page 66 identifies the Matérn exponent, in the Laplace variable, with the exponent of that kernel "with ratio q² and constant 2c²", and S-39 repeats it. In other words the kernel μ_t of the Matérn pyramid is Brownian motion run at a random time whose law is Lindeberg's limit kernel, the V_t of Theorem 5.4(1). This is the nearest precedent for contribution 3 in the journal's own literature. It appears nowhere in the abstract or in "Relation to the literature" (p. 5), where a reader is told only that such sequences of implicit steps "occur in nonstationary iterated Tikhonov regularization". The family is not new; its selection by the stage lemma, and its reading as a pyramid, are. Section 1 has to say so.

### 6.3 The other items

- **Hanke and Groetsch (1998); Radmoser, Scherzer and Weickert (2000).** The positioning (p. 5, p. 111) is fair and hedged ("were not found in that literature"). One addition: that a Tikhonov step is an implicit Euler step and obeys an extremum principle is itself in that literature, so the sufficiency half of the stage lemma is known in this reading too, not only as "the classical maximum principle of an elliptic equation" (p. 80).
- **Mallat (1989).** "The axiomatic dyadic neighbour, by projection rather than smoothing" (p. 5), and the comparison of the knot relation with the scaling relation (S-43): fair.
- **Whittle (1954); Lindgren, Rue and Lindström (2011).** Cited for the name and the covariance, correctly (p. 5, p. 58, p. 108). The point of the second paper, that the grid counterpart of (κ² − Δ)^γ is a sparse operator with a Markov structure, is the subject of Section 6.4, where it is cited for the covariance only.
- **Cavaretta, Dahmen and Micchelli (1991).** I could not check the anchors of p. 41. Proposition 6.16 is very likely folklore of that field: a trigonometric polynomial that is radial near 0 is constant, and this holds for signed masks as well. Cite it, or say that it was not found.

### 6.4 The stage lemma (outside the block; one remark)

Proposition 5.18(3) is presented as belonging "to the author's work ... not yet published" (p. 80) and is not positioned. Its content is that of a classical theorem: the principle says that A := (μ∗)^-1 − I, on the range of μ∗, satisfies Au(x) ≥ 0 at every local maximum x of u, and a translation-invariant operator with the positive maximum principle is of Lévy type (Courrège), local exactly when the principle holds at local and not only at global maxima. The finite-step, convolution form proved here is elementary and self-contained, and I verified it; it should still be placed against that theorem, because that is where a reader will look for it.

## 7. Exposition and structure (the block)

**7.1 Where the argument is hard to follow.**

- The contributions list (pp. 2–4) runs to two and a half pages and paraphrases some twenty-five statements. It is too long to orient and too compressed to inform. The paragraph on the three senses of "exact" (p. 3) is the worst case: the senses are fixed in Definition 6.2, eighty-five pages later, and the introduction uses them before a reader can know why three are needed.
- Notation (pp. 14–15). The symbol table records that D has five meanings, σ four, L three and μ two; D_c and T_c are two names for one dilation; the Gaussian coefficient has three normalizations (a in ⟨ω, Aω⟩, Sato's A^S = 2A, the a_0 of the origin data) and the Lévy measure two (ν and the folded ϖ, twice ν on the half-line).
